# Book One — Canto 1: From the Spire

*Opening Arc*

---

The winter wind bit through the woollen cloak as Vyaan adjusted the heavy fabric around his shoulders, the dark blue material embroidered with silver thread that caught what little light the grey morning offered. His leather boots crunched against the frost-covered stones as he secured his travel pack, breath forming small clouds in the frigid air. The fur-lined collar of his riding coat provided some warmth against his neck, but nothing could ward off the chill that had settled deep in his bones after his time in the mountain's shadow.

As he approached the iron gates of the Spire's grounds, his steps slowed. There, standing motionless as carved stone, was the Sage. The old master's face bore no expression. Just the same granite stillness that had greeted Vyaan on his first day as a frightened boy of thirteen.

He stopped before his teacher, unsure whether to speak or wait. The silence stretched between them like a blade.

"You are ready," Kaelvar said finally, his voice carrying no warmth, only statement.

"Master, I—"

"Do not thank me." The sage's blue eyes fixed on him. Vyaan straightened involuntarily. "What I have given you was not kindness, but necessity."

Kaelvar stepped closer. For the first time in years, Vyaan saw something flicker beneath that stoic exterior, something distant and contemplative. Nothing on the sage's face moved except his lips, as if even speech was a concession his body grudged to make. "Your father once stood where you stand now," the master continued, his voice dropping lower. "I was... different then. Less thorough in my methods. I believed that natural talent could substitute for discipline, that charm could replace the harder virtues. Back then, I still believed a crown could ripen a boy into a man."

The wind howled through the mountain pass, but neither man moved.

"With you," Kaelvar's words came slowly, deliberately, "I left no stone unturned. Every weakness, I found and forged into strength. Every comfort, I stripped away until only discipline remained. I taught you not just to fight, but to endure. Not just to lead, but to sacrifice."

The sage's weathered hand touched the iron gate, fingers tracing the cold metal.

"The world shifts beneath our feet, Prince Vyaan. The trials your generation shall inherit may demand virtues unknown to easier ages." His eyes pierced Vyaan's. "What you have learned here you should hold fast. For only it may prove the only currency worth possessing in the days to come."

As the words settled between them, something fundamental shifted in Vyaan. Until this moment, he had been a vessel being filled. Now, suddenly, terribly, he understood he was complete. The boy who had arrived in these mountains years ago felt like a ghost, a half-formed thing that had never truly existed. Only now—standing in the cold wind, meeting his master's unflinching stare—did he feel the full gravity of his own existence.

Only today had he become a man.

Kaelvar stepped back, his large hands interlocking behind him once more. Time made a full circle. Here stood the same unyielding sage who had received him years ago with the same rigid posture and the same stone mask. Only now, Vyaan understood what he was looking at.

"Now go. Amravan waits its prince."

---

As Vyaan mounted his horse and rode toward the winding path that would take him down from the Iron Spire, he felt the weight of his teacher's words settling on his shoulders like a second cloak—heavier and colder than the first, filled with implications he couldn't quite grasp.

The frost-covered path wound down from the Iron Spire like a serpent's spine, each hoofbeat of Vyaan's mount echoing against the silent peaks. He had ridden perhaps half a league beyond the Spire's ancient boundaries when a figure emerged from behind a cluster of wind-bent pines.

The man was young, near Vyaan's own age, but carried himself with the rigid bearing of a seasoned soldier. Dark curls framed a hawk's face, all sharp angles and watchful eyes that tracked everything. His dark cloak bore no insignia, yet everything about his stance spoke of military precision. His face held the same unyielding quality as Kaelvar's, as if emotion were a luxury he had long since abandoned.

"Prince Vyaan," the stranger said, his voice flat and formal. "I am Asher, ordained by High Commander Eitan Uriel of the Aionēkh Order to serve as your shadow."

Vyaan pulled his horse to a halt, studying this unexpected escort. "My shadow?"

"The roads beyond the protected walls are no longer safe for one of your stations to travel alone." Asher's hand rested casually on the pommel of his sword, a gesture that seemed both natural and perpetually ready. "The High Commander deemed it necessary."

A cold knot formed in Vyaan's stomach, one that had nothing to do with the mountain air. "Not safe? From what?" He leaned forward in his saddle. "Who would dare threaten Aravaan? What force exists that we should fear?"

The man didn't answer.

Instead, he turned to the road and said, "The Crown's Abode awaits your return, my prince. We should make haste, the journey is not long, but daylight grows short."

"That's not an answer." Vyaan's voice carried the authority he had learned to wield at the Spire, but it seemed to have no effect on his stoic companion.

"It is the answer I can give," Asher replied, already turning his own mount toward the winding road. "Come. A few hours' ride will see us within the capital's walls before the sun sets.

---

The path had widened as they descended, allowing Vyaan's horse to fall into an easy rhythm behind Asher's mount. Vyaan had grown comfortable enough to lean back against his horse's hindquarters, his legs still hooked around the saddle, but his posture relaxed for the first time in months. The winter sun, though weak, felt warm on his face after years of the Spire's perpetual shadow.

A jay darted overhead, its blue wings catching the light as it swooped toward them. Before Vyaan could even register the bird's presence, Asher's scarred hand had moved to his blade, his eyes tracking the creature's flight until it veered away into the pines.

"You're watching birds now?" Vyaan asked, amused despite himself.

Asher's gaze never left the treeline ahead. "I watch everything."

"Right." Vyaan shifted slightly, studying the rigid line of his escort's shoulders. "And how exactly do I know you're who you claim to be? Anyone could say they serve the High Commander."

Without turning or breaking his vigilant scan of their surroundings, Asher pulled back his left sleeve. Crimson ink marked his forearm: a compass rose crowned by a radiant sun, ocean waves curling along the outer ring. The mark of the Aionēkh, burned into flesh.

"Satisfied?" Asher asked.

"I suppose." Vyaan settled back again, watching the clouds drift overhead. "You ever trained under Kaelvar?"

"No."

"Good. You should thank gods for this fortune." He sighed, half-laughing. "You know, when I first arrived, I thought it'd be some glorious warrior's path. Heroes, legends, brotherhood. All that myth. Instead, I get a crypt-keeper with a stick. I swear Kaelvar was trying to kill me slowly. Every single morning before dawn—sword work until my arms felt like lead, practicing the same seventeen forms over and over until I could execute them in my sleep. Then he'd make me do them with my eyes closed. Then blindfolded while he threw stones at me to test my balance."

Asher said nothing. He pressed his mare forward with his thighs and the animal responded to the subtle command. The reins hung loose in his gloved hands as they rounded the bend, leather swaying with each step.

"Then came strategy sessions that lasted until my head pounded," Vyaan continued, warming to his subject. "Ancient battles, supply lines, terrain advantages—all of it crammed into my skull while I was still dizzy from morning drills. And if I got a single detail wrong about some war that happened two centuries ago, he'd make me copy the entire tactical manual by hand. Twice."

The silence stretched between them, broken only by the steady clip-clop of hooves on stone.

"And the endurance runs—gods, those runs. Every afternoon, full gear, up and down those mountain paths until my lungs burned and my legs shook. Rain, snow, blazing sun, didn't matter. If I stumbled even once, he'd add another league to the distance. I once ran for six hours straight because I had the audacity to pause for water without permission."

A squirrel chittered in a nearby tree. Asher's head whipped toward the sound, his entire attention converging on the small animal like a blade finding its mark. His eyes narrowed. The squirrel froze mid-chirp, caught in a stare that had felled men.

"But the cruellest part," Vyaan's voice carried years of pent frustration, "was not even the brutality itself. However flawlessly I performed, however perfectly I executed his lessons, however brilliantly I solved his war games—he would discover some minute flaw to make me suffer for. I once disarmed three of his assistant masters in succession, my blade dancing precisely as he had taught me. His response? He had me scrub the training grounds with a brush meant for mail rings, said I had grown 'too satisfied with myself' and that such pride would be my ruin."

Vyaan shook his head, lost in the memory. "The man could find fault with sunrise if he set his mind to it. I'd execute a perfect cavalry charge simulation, and he'd criticize the angle of my sword grip. I'd solve a siege problem that had stumped students for decades, and he'd make me solve ten more because I'd smiled when I figured it out."

Asher continued his silent vigil of the path ahead.

"Are you even listening?" Vyaan asked.

"I'm listening."

"Could have fooled me. Here I am, finally free of that stone-faced tyrant, and you're acting just like—"

Vyaan sat bolt upright in his saddle, his easy demeanour vanishing as a sudden thought struck him. Spine straightened as though some invisible hand had pulled a cord through his vertebrae, snapping him taut.

"What if you're not from the Crimson Order at all?" His voice had gone cold, all trace of complaint replaced by the sharp edge Kaelvar had honed. "What if you're a trap? Here to kill me on some lonely mountain path where no one would find my body until spring?"

Asher finally turned his head slightly, not fully looking back but acknowledging the question. "If I wanted you dead, Prince, you'd have died while lying on your horse complaining about sword drills."

The simple, matter-of-fact delivery made it more convincing than any oath. Vyaan straightened fully in his saddle. "Anyway. If you're planning to kill me, at least wait until I've eaten." He frowned, the levity draining from his face.

"If you're meant to be my shadow, shouldn't you be riding behind me?"

"Hard to watch for threats ahead if I'm staring at your horse's tail," Asher replied without turning. "I lead, you follow. You stay alive."

Vyaan made a face at the back of Asher's head. "You know, for someone named Asher, you're remarkably grim. Means 'blessed and happy', doesn't it?"

If Asher had any response to that observation, he kept it to himself as they continued down the mountain path.

Vyaan continued his litany of complaints about life at the Kaelvar's, recounting endless drills, impossible standards in the relentless pursuit of perfection in every conceivable discipline. Asher remained stoic, offering nothing more than occasional grunts while his eyes swept the surrounding countryside.

The mountain path descended gradually toward the valley floor.

By midday, a sight rose before them that made Vyaan's breath catch. Eight years he had carried this image in his mind, and now it bloomed before him in full—the seven magnificent domes of the capital, rising like golden crowns across the ancient Reivi River. Distance had not dimmed their splendour. They gleamed beyond the massive stone arches of the Barānmōr Dam, as eternal as the mountains behind him.

"Almost home," he whispered, not sure if the tightness in his chest was anticipation or dread.
